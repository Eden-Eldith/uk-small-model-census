\section{Introduction}
An inventory can miss working AI capability before any assessment of that
capability has begun. If its entry condition is a registered commercial
entity, an individual who trains and releases a model without incorporating
a company is outside its sampling frame. The resulting omission is a
property of the inventory's design. It does not tell us whether that
individual's work exists, is discoverable, or has research value.

The UK government's \emph{Artificial Intelligence Sector Study 2025},
published on 29 October 2026, measures an explicitly commercial population.
Its inclusion rules require a UK-registered business reporting to Companies
House and active commercial AI provision. The methodology excludes
universities, charities, networks and non-registered individual contractors
\cite{sectorstudy}. That is a consequential boundary when a sector inventory
is used as a starting point for understanding domestic technical capability:
research and released models can exist outside the entities it counts.

A separate Council for Science and Technology (CST) recommendation calls for
mapping existing UK AI activity across computing, data, skills, models,
governance and adoption \cite{cst}. Its July 2026 letter, published in October,
also discusses domestic smaller models and a proposed public-interest base
model from the Alan Turing Institute. A map organised around existing
capability therefore needs a route to discover builders who do not appear
in company inventories. This study implements one such route, beginning
with released models and following their public provenance to people.

The immediate motivation was the author's experience of making WiggleGPT,
a small language model pretrained from scratch in Gosport, publicly
available with code, weights and a paper \cite{wigglegpt}. Earlier
cross-platform research supplied to the author had not identified another
case meeting a narrower combination of British residence, independent
funding, personal consumer hardware and original architectural research.
That history motivates the question; those prior reports are not counted
as observations in the present dataset. Here the operational question is
broader and explicitly geographic: who can be found behind every model
returned by a specified public discovery query, and what UK connections
do the available sources establish?

The contribution is a complete, dated repository cohort, a source-linked
location table, and model-level interpretation of UK-connected releases.
The outputs make each inclusion and unresolved case inspectable. They
also expose the difference between a catalogue filter, a documented
training run and an independent research contribution.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Capability discovery needs an entry point for individuals}
The existing company inventory and the proposed capability map answer
different questions. The former has an explicit registration and commercial
activity boundary; the latter concerns the resources and skills available
to the country. A released model is a practical entry point into the second
question. Its documentation can lead to an individual, a small research
group, a company or a university without deciding in advance that only
one of those forms of organisation can produce useful work.

The distinction matters even when a model is small. Writing a training
system, preparing data, running pretraining, modifying an architecture and
releasing usable artefacts exercise different capabilities from consuming
a hosted model. The scale of the checkpoint does not erase those
capabilities. Equally, a standard-architecture learning project, a task
fine-tune and an original-architecture pretraining experiment should be
described in their own terms. Keeping these attributes in separate columns
lets readers find the kind of work relevant to their purpose.

\subsection{What this means for sovereign AI}
Domestic capability includes people able to build, inspect and adapt models.
It also includes their accumulated experimental knowledge and the public
artefacts through which others can examine or reuse it. This is the aspect
of sovereignty that a repository census can illuminate. The CST's
recommendation makes existing activity and dependencies relevant to
decision-making; the present data provide a small, concrete input to that
task rather than treating incorporation as a prerequisite for visibility.

The census does not reduce sovereignty to an author's location. A model can
be built in Britain while using imported chips, international software,
foreign-origin datasets or a tokenizer developed elsewhere. A useful map
records those dependencies alongside domestic authorship and control. It
can then distinguish local technical capability, ownership and public
availability from claims about independence across the entire supply chain.

\subsection{Discovery has a practical purpose}
The release is designed to support action on completed work. A reader can
follow a row to the model, inspect the documented contribution and identify
its creator. That creates a route to technical collaboration, paid work,
compute support, sponsorship or a relevant introduction. It also allows an
institution building a capability inventory to include public research
without first asking its creator to establish a company or propose a
different project. This study records no resulting funding, employment or
compute allocation; it supplies the evidence and discovery route through
which those outcomes could become possible.

\subsection{A finite search can return positive evidence}
The motivating catalogue contained fewer than one thousand models. Every
matching identifier could be enumerated, and each of its 495 publishing
accounts could be examined. This establishes a concrete alternative to
answering an existence question with a familiar list of institutions.
The affirmative results are directly inspectable; unresolved locations
remain visible rather than being converted into claims of non-existence.
Future additions can extend the same record instead of forcing the next
researcher to repeat the discovery work from an empty starting point.

\section{Limitations}
The result is complete for the collected query, not for all UK model
development. Metadata choices, missing tags, deleted or private repositories,
other hosting platforms and the parameter ceiling determine what is
outside the frame. Current self-reported location may differ from location
at training time. A publisher may be a republisher, and a model may have
co-authors whose locations are not documented. The release preserves such
distinctions, but does not establish the residence of every contributor.

Location research used one assigned AI-assisted review per account, followed
by consolidation and inspection of the UK findings. It was not a blinded
multi-rater experiment and no inter-rater reliability statistic is claimed.
Search failures and public pages with sparse information contribute to the
unknown category. Model training claims are attributed to their published
documentation; weights were not downloaded and training runs were not
replicated for this census. Absent documentation of funding or hardware
ownership is recorded as unknown.

The author's own model is in the sampling frame and was part of the
motivation. Its evidence is reported alongside the other cases, with the
same separation between location, training origin, architecture and
resources. The disclosed relationship allows readers to assess that
interest directly.

\section{Conclusion}
A public model catalogue can reveal UK technical work that a company-based
inventory is not designed to contain. Enumerating the full query and
following the releases to their documented creators produces a reusable
record of both findings and unresolved cases. A broader map of UK AI
capability can incorporate this kind of evidence now: released work,
named builders, public sources and explicit dependencies.
