\section{Capability that existing inventories may not see}
British AI sovereignty depends on identifying the people who can already
design, train and develop models in the United Kingdom. An independent
researcher who builds a language model from randomly initialised parameters
has created domestic technical capability, whether the work was performed
in an academic laboratory, a funded company or a home workshop. If national
mapping begins with registered businesses and university networks, such
individuals need not appear in the initial population at all.

The government's \emph{Artificial Intelligence Sector Study 2025}, published
on 29 October 2026, measures commercial AI activity through UK-registered
companies. Its methods already use frontier language models and agentic
research tools for discovering and classifying firms. Its stated population
excludes non-registered individual contractors \cite{sectorstudy}.
\textbf{The absence of an independent developer from a company census is
a consequence of its population definition, not evidence that the developer
does not exist.} That boundary matters when an economic inventory becomes
the starting point for understanding national technical capability.

The Council for Science and Technology's advice, published on 19 October
2026, calls for a Sovereignty Plan informed by a comprehensive view of
the domestic AI ecosystem \cite{cst}. Will that map search Hugging Face,
GitHub, Zenodo and individual builders, or reproduce the institutions it
already knows? The practical question is who has already built domestic
models outside university and institutional routes, and how to find them.

I undertook this study because I wanted to find other people building
language models independently in Britain. I trained WiggleGPT, approximately 124 million parameters, from scratch
in Gosport on personally owned consumer GPUs and published its weights,
code and research \cite{wigglegpt,wigglecard}. Six earlier manual and
AI-assisted investigations did not identify another matching independent
builder. I do not believe that I am the only one. I want to know who else
is already doing this work, who could benefit from support, and what we
could build together.

That is why I began with published models. A released checkpoint, its
training code and its development record provide a way to find capability
before its builder has joined a recognised institution or formed a company.
The practical purpose of this study is to identify people and projects that
research teams, compute providers and public bodies can approach for
collaboration. Someone already working from a home workshop should be able
to contribute from that starting point. Recognition should connect existing
work to an opportunity to develop it.

\section{Discoverable models, undiscovered builders}
WiggleGPT is publicly available with weights, code and an archived paper.
The \emph{State of AI Report} presents itself as a widely read and trusted
independent annual overview of AI progress \cite{stateofaiabout}.
Its 2026 edition, dated 8 October and covering the preceding
12 months, contains no mention of WiggleGPT or my name in its searchable
text \cite{stateofai2026}. The earliest saved Zenodo record gives an original
publication date of 25 November 2025 and a deposit created on 8 December
2025 \cite{wiggleoriginal}. The work therefore predates the report and falls
within its stated reporting window. The omission concerns the already
released original work.

I found the same problem in ordinary searches. Generic searches for AI
research in Gosport often returned institutional pages or jobs instead of
my work. Searching for the exact name WiggleGPT found it. A discovery
system that requires knowing the model's name in advance cannot identify
unknown builders by their capability. Yahoo surfaced my Grokipedia entry
where Google did not; I read
that as relevant results being downranked. In earlier agent-assisted
searches, broad requests for independent British model builders produced
answers that people fitting the description did not exist, supported by
familiar official archives. When I directed agents to Hugging Face using
these filters, they found WiggleGPT immediately. The answer changed when
the discovery route changed.

I have deliberately linked my public research identity, hometown and
model. That voluntary public connection should make the work discoverable
without requiring a newspaper profile or a publicity campaign. The
technical record is already there to be found.

When discovery begins and ends with institutional networks, it becomes a
closed loop: the organisations already recognised by the state identify
people already connected to those organisations. The sector study already
uses language models and agentic tools to discover firms
\cite{sectorstudy}. Giving an agent a company-centred population still leaves
an independent person outside that population. Automating the search does
not change whom it is designed to find.

Breaking that loop requires following the work itself: Hugging Face for
models, GitHub for implementations and Zenodo for research outputs. These
are ordinary places where independent builders make their work available.
The route into the capability map should begin with what someone has built,
so that university affiliation, company formation and personal connections
do not determine whether the search reaches them.

Discovery spending should be judged by whether it reaches people beyond
the networks already represented in the results. Reproducing a list of
university contacts at public expense does not solve the missing-population
problem. Selection also needs to recognise demonstrated ability outside
the familiar social and institutional profile of a funded researcher.
Someone who can build the model should not first have to become the
expected kind of applicant for their work to count.

\section{From inventory to opportunity}
Discovery becomes useful when it connects demonstrated capability to
opportunities to develop it. A public index of
independent builders would let research groups, compute providers, companies
and public bodies find documented work, identify collaborators and determine
where support could accelerate it. Technical capability should be
discoverable before a researcher learns to market a company or builds a
large social-media audience.

\subsection{A practical route from learning to research}
The educational task is to turn passive consumers of AI into active
producers. A learner who has trained a model can begin to understand the
technology as something they can construct and change. A small Shakespeare
model provides a concrete introduction: initialise the weights, watch the
training process, generate text, change the model and see what happens.
School and after-school projects could use that experience to introduce
Python, PyTorch and GPU computing. Building a working text generator makes
the connection between code, data, hardware and learned behaviour visible.
That is a practical way to demystify AI.

Some access routes already exist. Kaggle offers free, quota-limited GPU
notebooks, and Google's AI subscriptions include premium Colab benefits
\cite{kagglegpu,colab2026}. People need to know that these routes are
available and how to use them for building models. Schools should also
identify suitable graphics PCs already available in art, design and
computing departments and make them accessible to supervised research
clubs outside teaching hours. Regional technical hubs could provide the
support and additional compute needed as projects grow.

That route should continue through machine-learning research
apprenticeships covering hosting, inference, fine-tuning, distributed
training and security. Learners need credit and a share in the value of
work they help to create. Where a project produces a patentable invention,
learners and their school should share in the returns under an agreed
arrangement. Those returns could help sustain the next round of local
projects, while a concrete stake in a working invention gives its builders
a reason to keep developing it. The national ambition should be widespread
competence in building AI, extending beyond familiarity with using chatbots
for everyday tasks.

Funding only today's best-established performers misses tomorrow's best.
In a field where tools and methods change rapidly, a newcomer can develop
an approach that established selection criteria never anticipated. A
competitive national programme needs that capacity for reinvention as
well as support for its current leaders. Developing the next researcher
requires a progression from an introductory model to sustained research,
supported by regional technical hubs and access to compute.
That is how producing AI can become an attainable technical activity for
more people in Britain.

Public investment creates a reciprocal relationship. People given the
opportunity to learn, access compute and develop their work should be
enabled and expected to contribute capability back to the country over
time. That contribution can take the form of useful systems, research,
training others or sustained technical work. The same principle applies
across educational and recruitment routes: support should build a lasting
domestic capability, and people need a practical route through which to
make that contribution.

My route combined practical hardware experience with self-directed
learning. I grew up helping in my father's computer shop and started
learning Python on 17 January 2025. I build and repair my own hardware;
that practical competence made it possible to keep the training machine
running for a sustained period. Hardware skills, persistence and the
ability to maintain a working system are part of the capability a national
map needs to recognise.

\subsection{The development loop changes who can build}
My research programme was assisted by AI throughout. I directed and
oversaw the work; AI agents implemented, trained and developed it under
that direction. For WiggleGPT's later task-adaptation experiments, I gave
Astra one instruction: make my established model do what GPT-1 had
demonstrated. Astra carried out the subsequent implementation and task
training. The resulting evaluations are documented in the public release
\cite{wigglecard}.
This is the model-assisted development loop described in
Section~\ref{sec:rsi}: I used existing AI systems to help build and improve
another model. Human direction, oversight and technical judgement were
part of that process. The ability to participate in it is already here.

That changes the discovery problem. Tools can give people practical
capabilities they previously would have found much harder to acquire.
A system that looks only for the credentials and institutional routes
associated with earlier generations of AI research can miss the people
who are now building through these new routes. Discovery therefore needs
to follow demonstrated work as that capability develops. The population
of capable builders changes with the tools available to them. An inventory
that searches only yesterday's routes into research will miss some of
today's capability and the people who could develop it further tomorrow.

Regional access extends the range of people who can participate. Gosport
already has a mapped data centre: Virgin Media Business's network map
lists iomart's DC9 Gosport \cite{vmbnetwork}. The town also has an
independent builder with a publicly released model. Connecting local
technical work to local infrastructure should be an ordinary task of
capability development.

At national scale, the programme should connect suitable, well-networked
data centres for distributed training and give independent builders access
to shared compute and collaborators. Match workloads to the available
accelerators, network bandwidth and latency, then put that capacity to
work. Regional technical hubs can connect schools, apprentices, engineers
and model builders to this effort. Electricity, cooling and long-term
infrastructure planning determine where it can be sustained. The purpose
is a national model-building programme in which capability outside the
familiar university and technology centres can contribute.

\subsection{Publication and participation}
Publication access is another part of discoverability. arXiv requires
endorsement for first submissions and new endorsement categories. Its
documented routes include claimed co-authored work together with a
qualifying institutional email, or personal endorsement by an established
arXiv author. Its guidance recommends approaching someone known personally
who understands the field \cite{arxivendorsement}. An independent builder
outside those networks has an additional access problem before the paper
can enter that discovery route. Zenodo and model repositories offer
additional routes for independent work to become public.
A national inventory should follow reproducible outputs across these venues
instead of treating participation in one academic publication route as the
boundary of research capability.

\subsection{A national programme with clear responsibilities}
These proposals connect discovery, development and retention. They require
an explicit division of responsibility:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=5pt]
\item \textbf{The Sovereign AI Fund:} find independent builders through
their released models, code and research; provide a route to support before
company formation; and assess existing domestic models for scaling with
national compute. Hardware grants, compute access and research
collaborations should connect demonstrated work to its next stage.
\item \textbf{DSIT, Parliament and public bodies:} connect regional
technical hubs and suitable data centres into a national development
programme, fund active testing of British systems, and retain the people
who hold sensitive technical knowledge through sustained research and
employment. Discovery must lead to support that lasts long enough to build
domestic capability.
\item \textbf{Schools, colleges and training providers:} teach practical
model-building, open suitable existing hardware to research clubs, and
establish machine-learning apprenticeships. Agree how learners receive
credit and share in the returns from inventions they help to create, so
that successful projects can sustain further local work.
\end{itemize}
The result should be a continuous route from a first working model to
research, collaboration and useful national capability. Each organisation
has a part to play in making that route work.

\section{What about the independents?}
I want to find the other builders, make their work visible and establish
what we could achieve together with sustained support. The people paid
to discover sovereign capability should be finding publicly released
independent work on the main model-hosting platform. Failing to do so is
\emph{a major indictment of their ability to capture sovereign AI talent
and capability}. Effective talent discovery must lead from an inventory
to collaboration, practical support and long-term technical capacity.

AI-assisted development makes that task continuous. Existing models help
people build further models; new tools bring new people into the work;
support determines where their subsequent development takes place. A
capability map must keep up with that changing population and connect its
discoveries to timely action.

The strategic argument is that the race through RSI to AGI and ASI will
determine who controls the technology on which other countries depend.
Britain must build during this transition. Accepting the position of a
customer risks becoming a vassal state; discovering and developing domestic
builders is one practical part of avoiding that outcome. The Hugging Face
incident already shows why access to a supplier's models can fail at the
moment independent action is most needed. National sovereignty requires
the people, models, infrastructure and retained knowledge to act.

\begin{samepage}
Britain's negotiating position improves when it can produce the technology
on which it depends. Becoming a producer of AI includes teaching people
to build it, recognising what they already produce, connecting them to
resources and retaining their expertise. \textbf{If Britain wants to know what sovereign AI capability it already has, it must look not only where companies and universities report it, but where independent individuals actually build and publish it.}\par
\end{samepage}
